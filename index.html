Who is inside the circles: ages

Circle ACircle B

Five age bands taken from the 2017 census at tehsil level, the smallest area with public age data, and blended by how many people the circle holds in each tehsil. Treat it as an area average, not a head count, and remember it predates the flood by five years.

Tehsils a circle touches are tinted in its colour: the deeper the tint, the more of that tehsil's 2 km cells sit inside the circle.

The numbers inside each circle

IndicatorAB
Seen from space. The indigo layer is floodwater detected by satellite between 1 July and 31 August 2022 (UNOSAT, VIIRS, pixels of about 375 m). It is a first pass that nobody has checked on the ground, and cloud, tree cover or dense housing can hide water. "In the water" below simply means inside this layer.
Computed, not observed. The amber hatched layers and depth bands come from a global river-flood model run for 1-in-10, 1-in-100 and 1-in-500-year floods. They show what such a flood could do, not what 2022 did, and they sit in their own amber rows below.
Handle with care. People per cell are WorldPop's 2020 estimate, not a count. OpenStreetMap barely covers rural Pakistan, so building counts are minimums, and the building method falls back to the cell centre wherever no building is mapped.

    A circle adds up every 2 km cell (built from the 100 m grid) whose centre falls inside it. Method A scales each 100 m cell by how much of it is under water; B counts a cell whole if its centre is wet; C shares a cell's people among its flooded mapped buildings, and uses B where none are mapped.

    The all-tehsils column adds up the 2 km cells and is within 0.1% of the project totals (cells with under one person and pixels outside tehsil outlines are not included); the project totals are on the evidence page.

    The modelled layers, the flooded-building points and the 2 km cells are simplified or aggregated copies made for the browser. All figures are computed from the full-resolution data. Full detail and limits: How we know.
